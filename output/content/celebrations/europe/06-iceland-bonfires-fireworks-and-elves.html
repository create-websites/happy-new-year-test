Iceland: Bonfires, Fireworks, and Elves

Iceland's New Year glows against one of the longest nights of the year. Reykjavík neighborhoods gather around community bonfires called áramótabrennur, then people turn toward the sky as fireworks illuminate the winter darkness. The experience feels unusually participatory because much of the display is created by residents.

🇮🇸 Iceland🎊 January 1 traditions🌍 Around the world

What makes the celebration special

Bonfires bring friends and neighbors together to sing, talk and make a symbolic break with the old year. Fireworks fundraising has also been associated with Icelandic volunteer rescue organizations. Icelandic folklore adds stories of hidden people and elves to the season's atmosphere, but those beliefs should be treated as cultural storytelling rather than something all Icelanders practice. Say Gleðilegt nýtt ár when the year begins.

Where to experience it

Walk past Reykjavík's striking Hallgrímskirkja and colorful central streets before choosing a designated public viewing place. The Blue Lagoon can be part of a broader Iceland visit, though its access and opening status must be checked separately from the holiday festivities. Views of fireworks also depend heavily on weather.

Planning a memorable New Year visit

Expect cold winds, icy pavements and limited public transport late at night. Arrange a return trip in advance, keep distance from fireworks and check official bonfire locations. If you travel for northern lights as well, remember that cloud cover and solar activity make sightings uncertain.

Good to know before you go

Fireworks are not the only way Icelanders celebrate; quieter gatherings are common, and outdoor plans can change quickly with severe weather.

Holiday plans are affected by weather, permits, crowd limits, local customs and transport schedules. Use this guide to understand the tradition, then confirm exact details for the year of your trip with the responsible event organizer or tourism authority.

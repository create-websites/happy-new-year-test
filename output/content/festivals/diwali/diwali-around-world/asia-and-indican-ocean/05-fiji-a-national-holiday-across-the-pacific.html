Fiji: A National Holiday Across the Pacific

In Fiji, Diwali is part of the country's shared public calendar, reflecting generations of Indo-Fijian history. Clay lamps, illuminated homes and visits with neighbors give the Festival of Lights a special place in a Pacific island society.

FijiNational Diwali holidayIndo-Fijian heritage

The story behind the celebration

Indentured workers brought religious, musical and food traditions from the Indian subcontinent in the colonial period, and their descendants developed vibrant local Diwali customs. Families clean and decorate homes, worship, share sweets and often welcome guests beyond their own religious community.

What makes this Diwali celebration distinctive?

The glow of home decorations in Suva and Nadi can matter more than any one ticketed show. Temple communities and cultural groups may host programs, but scale varies each season. Look for local accounts of Girmit history to understand why Diwali is also a story of resilience and belonging.

Food, family and community

Indo-Fijian cooking blends familiar festival sweets with local tastes. Ladoo, barfi and savory snacks are common choices, though every household has its own menu. A shared plate or neighborly visit can carry the celebration across cultural boundaries.

Where to experience it

Consider Suva for museums and heritage context, or Nadi for a broader trip with access to local markets. Ask organizers before photographing religious services, and avoid confusing public buildings with open-access worship spaces.

How to plan responsibly

Fiji's government lists Diwali as a public holiday, observed on 9 November in 2026. Confirm transport, business and school schedules before making plans; local observances may begin on the evening before the holiday.

Important cultural context

Diwali's national recognition does not imply that every Fijian observes it religiously; shared celebrations coexist with Fiji's many faiths.

Gudi Padwa, Ugadi and Cheti Chand

Compare three spring New Year traditions—Gudi Padwa, Ugadi and Cheti Chand—and see how shared calendar rhythms become distinct regional celebrations.

Gudi Padwa Hub
Why this guide matters

Gudi Padwa is both a calendrical New Year and a living cultural tradition. These guides separate mythology, symbolism, history and astronomical context so readers can appreciate the festival without flattening its many layers.

One season, several New Year traditions

Across India, the arrival of Chaitra brings more than one New Year celebration. Maharashtra’s Gudi Padwa, the Ugadi traditions of Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, and the Sindhi festival of Cheti Chand all cluster around the spring New Year season, yet each has its own religious emphasis, food, language, symbols, and community memory.

The similarities show a shared calendrical world; the differences show how strongly Indian festivals are shaped by region.

Gudi Padwa in Maharashtra

Gudi Padwa is immediately recognizable because of the Gudi raised outside the home. Families may make rangoli, wear festive Maharashtrian dress, listen to the new panchang, eat a neem-jaggery preparation, and serve dishes such as puran poli or shrikhand.

The festival is both domestic and public. In cities and towns, processions, dhol-tasha, traditional dress, and community gatherings often add a civic dimension to the New Year.

Ugadi in the Deccan

Ugadi marks the New Year in several southern states. A famous culinary symbol is Ugadi pachadi, a mixture that combines multiple tastes. Like Maharashtra’s neem-jaggery custom, it is often interpreted as a reminder that a year contains varied experiences rather than sweetness alone.

House cleaning, rangoli or muggu, festive food, temple visits, and panchanga shravana are common parts of the day, though exact practices vary by language and region.

Cheti Chand in Sindhi tradition

Cheti Chand is a major Sindhi festival associated with the New Year and devotion to Jhulelal. Community gatherings, processions, devotional music, lamps, water symbolism, and shared meals often play an important role.

For Sindhi communities living across India and the global diaspora, Cheti Chand is also an important marker of cultural continuity and collective identity.

Celebrate comparison without flattening difference

It is tempting to call these festivals “the same New Year under different names,” but that loses important detail. They may share calendar proximity and themes of renewal, yet each community has developed its own stories, symbols, foods and devotional practices.

A good comparative celebration can acknowledge the shared springtime rhythm while learning the proper name, greeting, food, and ritual vocabulary of each tradition.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions

Are Gudi Padwa and Ugadi the same festival?

They are closely related regional New Year observances, but their customs, foods, languages and symbols differ.

What makes Cheti Chand distinct?

Cheti Chand is a Sindhi New Year festival strongly associated with Jhulelal and Sindhi community traditions.

Do all three occur on exactly the same date every year?

They often fall in the same New Year season, but observance can depend on regional calendrical conventions and local panchangs.

Halloween Candy Guide: Popular Treats, Candy Traditions and Creative Ways to Use Leftovers

Americans were projected to spend $4.1 billion on Halloween candy in 2026. Here's how to choose it, how much to buy, where the traditions came from : and what to do with the leftovers.

Key takeaways

  • The NRF projected $4.1 billion in Halloween candy spending for 2026; 96% of celebrants planned to buy candy.
  • Budget about 3 pieces per trick-or-treater, and estimate from previous years.
  • Candy corn dates to the 1880s; bite-size "fun-size" bars came with the postwar trick-or-treat boom.
  • Leftovers → baking, candy bark, science experiments, donations or buyback programs.

Candy is the currency of Halloween. For one night a year, children collect it door to door, adults hide the best pieces for themselves, and the whole country goes through a staggering quantity of sugar. The National Retail Federation projected Americans would spend $4.1 billion on Halloween candy in 2026, with 96% of those celebrating planning to buy some.

A short history of Halloween candy

Early Halloween treats weren't candy at all. Souling and guising traditions rewarded children with soul cakes, fruit, nuts and coins (see Trick-or-Treating Explained). Homemade popcorn balls, cookies and caramel apples were common in early 20th-century America.

Three developments made candy king:

  1. Candy corn (1880s). The tri-colored kernel was created in the 1880s and is commonly credited to George Renninger of Philadelphia's Wunderlee Candy Company. It was made by hand, pouring sugary slurry into cornstarch molds in layers. Agricultural novelty candies were popular then, and candy corn's harvest look made it a natural fit for autumn.
  2. The postwar boom. After World War II sugar rationing ended, candy makers aimed products squarely at trick-or-treaters with individually wrapped, bite-size pieces : what later became known as "fun size."
  3. Safety fears. From the 1970s, concerns about homemade treats pushed households toward factory-sealed candy, cementing commercial candy as the Halloween standard.

Perennial favorites

Rankings vary by survey, region and year, but certain categories always come out on top:

Chocolate bars The undisputed gold standard of the treat bag
Peanut-butter cups A national favorite (not suitable for nut allergies)
Chewy fruit candies Bright colors and tangy flavors
Sour candies Popular with older kids and teens
Lollipops and hard candy Long-lasting, inexpensive
Candy corn Loved and hated in equal measure

How much candy should you buy?

A practical formula:

Expected visitors × 3 pieces = pieces to buy.

How to estimate visitors:

  • Count last year : keep a tally this year for next time.
  • Ask neighbors who have lived there longer.
  • Consider the calendar: in 2026 Halloween falls on a Saturday, so expect more and later visitors.
  • Destination neighborhoods with famous decorations can see hundreds or thousands of kids; see America's Best Halloween Neighborhoods.

Keep a reserve bag of non-perishable treats : anything left over keeps for a while.

Allergy-friendly and inclusive treats

About one in thirteen U.S. children has a food allergy, according to Food Allergy Research & Education (FARE). You can include everyone:

  • Display a teal pumpkin to signal non-food treats are available.
  • Offer a separate bowl of stickers, glow sticks, mini notebooks, bubbles, temporary tattoos, pencils or small toys.
  • Choose candy labeled free of the top allergens.
  • Avoid latex balloons (latex allergy) and tiny toys for toddlers.

Candy etiquette

  • Kids: take one or two unless offered more; say thank you.
  • Hosts: keep the bowl stocked, and if you run out, turn off the porch light so kids know.
  • Self-serve bowls: a "please take two" sign helps but rarely survives the first teenager. A candy chute or staffed station is more reliable.

Sorting the haul

Back home, children love to sort and trade. It's also a good safety moment:

  • Discard anything unwrapped, homemade by strangers or with damaged packaging.
  • Remove choking hazards : hard candies, gum and small toys : for very young kids.
  • Separate allergens for children with allergies.
  • Set a plan: a few pieces a day lasts much longer than a sugar binge.

12 ways to use leftover candy

  1. Candy bark : scatter chopped candy over melted chocolate, chill, break.
  2. Cookie mix-ins : chop chocolate bars into cookie dough.
  3. Brownie surprises : bake peanut-butter cups into brownies.
  4. Ice-cream topping : chop and sprinkle; or blend into milkshakes.
  5. Trail mix : combine with pretzels, popcorn and cereal.
  6. Candy science : dissolve candy in water to watch colors spread, test which candies float or sink.
  7. Gingerbread house stash : save hard candies for December decorating.
  8. Advent calendar filling : freeze chocolate for December.
  9. Piñata filler : for upcoming birthday parties.
  10. Classroom rewards : sealed candy for teachers (check school policy).
  11. Donate : shelters, food banks and troop-support groups such as Operation Gratitude accept sealed candy (check current guidelines).
  12. Buyback programs : many dentists and orthodontists buy back candy in November, often donating it to troops or charity.

A note on teeth

Dentists suggest letting kids enjoy candy with meals rather than grazing all day, since frequency matters more than amount. Chocolate rinses away more easily than sticky or sour candies, and brushing before bed is the best habit of all.

Sweet takeaway

Candy is the heart of trick-or-treating for many families. Buy a little more than you think you need, include a few non-food treats for everyone, and turn the leftovers into a week of baking and experiments rather than a single sugar crash.

Questions & answers

How much Halloween candy should I buy?

Plan about three pieces per expected trick-or-treater. A typical suburban house might see anywhere from 30 to over 200 children; busy 'destination' neighborhoods can see many more. Buy a little extra or keep a backup bag of non-perishable treats.

What is the most popular Halloween candy?

Rankings vary by survey and region, but chocolate candy, especially chocolate bars and peanut-butter cups, consistently tops national lists, followed by chewy and fruity candies. Candy corn is famously divisive.

When was candy corn invented?

Candy corn was created in the 1880s. It is often credited to George Renninger of the Wunderlee Candy Company in Philadelphia and was later popularized by the Goelitz Confectionery Company.

What can I do with leftover Halloween candy?

Bake it into cookies and brownies, make candy bark, use it as an ice-cream topping, run candy science experiments, freeze chocolate for later, donate sealed candy to shelters or troop-support groups, or join a dentist's candy buyback program.

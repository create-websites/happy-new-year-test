Halloween Costume Safety: Visibility, Masks, Makeup and Comfortable Trick-or-Treating

The best costume is one a child can see out of, walk in and be seen in. A practical checklist for visibility, masks, makeup, fabrics and comfort on Halloween night.

Key takeaways

  • Be seen: add reflective tape and carry a flashlight or glow sticks : traffic is the biggest Halloween danger.
  • See clearly: swap vision-blocking masks for makeup, or push masks up while walking.
  • Fit and fabric: look for flame-resistant labels, hems above the ankle and real shoes.
  • Protect skin and eyes: patch-test face paint and skip non-prescription decorative contacts.

Halloween is a night of costumes, darkness and excited children on every sidewalk : a combination that calls for a little preparation. None of the advice below needs to dampen the fun. In fact, the safest costumes are often the most comfortable ones, which means kids can trick-or-treat longer and enjoy it more.

Rule 1: Be visible

The biggest danger on Halloween night isn't candy or monsters : it's traffic. Large numbers of children are crossing streets in the dark, often between parked cars, and many costumes are black. Studies of U.S. traffic data have found a higher risk of child pedestrian deaths on Halloween evening than on other nights.

Make every costume visible:

  • Reflective tape on the front, back and sides of costumes and treat bags. It's cheap, comes in black for dark costumes, and lights up in headlights.
  • Light sources: a flashlight, headlamp, light-up shoes or necklace, or glow sticks.
  • Bright treat bags or pumpkins rather than dark ones.
  • Bright colors where possible : or a light-colored accessory with a dark costume.

In 2026, Halloween falls on Saturday and daylight saving time ends the following morning, so most trick-or-treating will happen in full darkness after sunset around 6 p.m.

Rule 2: Be able to see

Masks are the classic costume problem. They restrict peripheral vision, slip out of place and can make breathing uncomfortable.

  • Use face paint or makeup instead whenever possible : Halloween Makeup Ideas has easy looks.
  • If wearing a mask, ensure eyeholes are large, it fits snugly and the child can see to both sides.
  • Push masks up or take them off when walking between houses or crossing the street.
  • Make sure hats, wigs and hoods don't slide over the eyes.

Rule 3: Choose safe materials

  • Look for flame-resistant labels on costumes, wigs and accessories. This doesn't mean fireproof, but these materials resist burning and extinguish more quickly.
  • Avoid loose, billowing fabrics around jack-o'-lanterns lit with real candles. Better still, encourage neighbors to use LED candles.
  • Teach kids to stop, drop and roll if clothing catches fire.

Rule 4: Get the fit right

Many Halloween injuries are simple trips and falls.

  • Hem costumes so they end above the ankle.
  • Wear real shoes that fit : not costume shoes, oversized boots or heels.
  • Avoid long trains, tails and capes that can catch on steps or tangle in legs; keep capes short.
  • Make sure the costume allows a full range of movement for climbing stairs.
  • Try it on a week ahead and walk around the house and yard.

Rule 5: Props should be soft and short

  • Swords, wands, broomsticks and pitchforks should be soft, flexible and short.
  • Avoid anything that could be mistaken for a real weapon.
  • Keep props light : kids end up carrying them all night.

Rule 6: Protect skin and eyes

Makeup

  • Use cosmetic-grade face paints that comply with FDA rules; never craft paints or markers.
  • Patch-test on the inner arm 24 hours before.
  • Remove makeup before bed to avoid skin irritation.

Contact lenses

Decorative "cat eye," "zombie" or colored lenses sold online or in costume shops without a prescription are illegal to sell that way in the U.S. and can cause corneal scratches, infections and permanent vision damage. If you want special lenses, get them prescribed and fitted by an eye doctor.

Fake blood and teeth

Choose mouth-safe products if they're near lips; check for allergies to latex in prosthetics and teeth.

Rule 7: Dress for the weather

Late October can be cold, wet or windy across much of the country.

  • Plan costumes that work over warm layers : or build the jacket into the costume.
  • Waterproof boots can be decorated to match.
  • Pack hand warmers and a light rain poncho.
  • In warm climates, ditch the heavy mask and bring water.

Comfort makes for a longer, happier night

Comfort is a safety feature. A child who is cold, can't see or keeps tripping will be cranky and distracted near roads.

  • Keep the costume light and the treat bag manageable.
  • Choose breathable fabrics.
  • Plan breaks and bring a snack and water.

Trick-or-treating safety checklist

  1. Children under about 12 go with an adult; older kids in groups with a planned route and check-in time.
  2. Stay on sidewalks; walk facing traffic if there are none.
  3. Cross at corners and crosswalks; never between parked cars.
  4. Visit only well-lit houses.
  5. Never enter a house or car for a treat.
  6. Sort candy at home and discard anything unwrapped or with torn packaging.
  7. Watch for kids with food allergies : look for teal pumpkins.

Learn more about the evolution of the night in Trick-or-Treating Explained, and consider a trunk-or-treat for younger children.

For drivers

  • Slow down in residential areas between about 5 and 9 p.m.
  • Watch for kids darting out from between parked cars.
  • Enter and leave driveways carefully.
  • Turn headlights on early.

A safe costume is a great costume

Reflective details can be woven into the design, makeup often looks better than a mask, and comfortable shoes mean more houses visited. A few small choices make Halloween night safer for everyone. For safety from the host's side, read Best Practices for a Trick-or-Treat-Friendly Home.

Questions & answers

Why is Halloween dangerous for pedestrians?

Halloween puts large numbers of children on streets after dark, often crossing between parked cars while distracted. Traffic-safety research has repeatedly found an elevated risk of child pedestrian deaths on Halloween evening compared with other nights, which is why visibility is the most important costume safety feature.

Are Halloween masks safe for kids?

Masks can block peripheral vision and make breathing uncomfortable. Many safety experts recommend face paint instead. If a child wears a mask, make sure the eyeholes are large, it fits snugly, and they push it up or remove it while walking and crossing streets.

Are decorative contact lenses safe for Halloween?

No. Eye-care experts and the FDA warn that decorative contacts sold without a prescription can cause scratches, infections and even vision loss. Only wear lenses prescribed and fitted by an eye-care professional.

What should a child carry for trick-or-treating?

A flashlight or headlamp, a glow stick or light-up accessory, a bright treat bag, a phone or contact card for older kids, and a light jacket if the evening is cold.

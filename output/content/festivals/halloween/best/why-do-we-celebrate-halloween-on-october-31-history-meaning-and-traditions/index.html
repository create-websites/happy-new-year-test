Why Do We Celebrate Halloween on October 31? History, Meaning and Traditions

Halloween is one of the few American holidays nailed to a calendar date instead of a convenient weekend. Here's why : and why that date has never budged.

Key takeaways

  • Halloween is the eve of All Saints' Day (November 1) : that's what All Hallows' Eve means.
  • Celtic days began at sunset, so Samhain festivities started on the evening of October 31.
  • Campaigns to move Halloween to the last Saturday in October have never caught on.
  • Halloween 2026 is a Saturday : the first since 2020 : and clocks fall back early on November 1.

Most American holidays have drifted to Mondays or been fixed to "the fourth Thursday" so that families get a predictable day off. Halloween is the stubborn exception. Whether it lands on a Tuesday school night or a perfect Saturday, it is always October 31. The reason has less to do with ghosts than with calendars : two of them, in fact.

The short answer: it's an "eve"

The word Halloween is a contraction of All Hallows' Even : the evening before All Hallows' Day, which is the old English name for All Saints' Day on November 1. "Hallow" simply meant a holy person or saint.

Christian feast days were traditionally preceded by a vigil or eve (think of Christmas Eve). As the Western Church settled All Saints on November 1 in the 8th and 9th centuries, the night of October 31 became its eve. The feast itself was followed by All Souls' Day on November 2, a day of prayer for the dead. Halloween is therefore the first night of a three-day season, Allhallowtide, devoted to saints and souls.

The older answer: Celtic days began at sunset

The Gaelic festival of Samhain, which predates Christianity in Ireland and Scotland, marked the end of harvest and the beginning of winter around November 1. The Celtic world, like many ancient cultures, counted a day from sunset to sunset. A festival "on November 1" therefore began on what we would call the evening of October 31.

Whether the Church deliberately placed All Saints over Samhain is still debated, but the two calendars lined up neatly. Both pointed to the same night as a threshold : between seasons, between years, and between the living and the dead. For the deeper history, see The Origins of Halloween.

Did you know? October 31 is also Reformation Day for many Protestants, marking the day in 1517 when Martin Luther is said to have posted his Ninety-Five Theses : chosen, historians believe, because crowds would gather for All Saints.

Why October suits a holiday about the dark

The date also feels right, which helps explain its staying power. In the Northern Hemisphere late October is when:

  • The harvest is finished. Pumpkins, squash, apples and corn are at their peak, which gave Halloween its food and its iconic lantern.
  • Nights lengthen quickly. Across most of the U.S., sunset on Halloween falls between about 5:45 and 6:40 p.m., so trick-or-treating happens in true darkness.
  • The landscape looks spooky. Bare branches, fog and early frost create a natural stage set that no decorator can match.

That seasonal mood is a big reason Southern Hemisphere countries, where October is spring, have been slower to adopt Halloween.

The campaign to move Halloween

Because October 31 often falls on a school night, there have been repeated calls to shift the celebration. The best-known push came in 2018, when the Halloween & Costume Association backed a petition to hold Halloween on the last Saturday of October. It argued that a weekend date would be safer, longer and less stressful for families. The petition drew wide attention and plenty of signatures, but nothing changed: no national body controls Halloween, and there is no law to amend.

Instead, communities have quietly solved the problem themselves. Many towns host parades, festivals and trunk-or-treat events on the nearest weekend, while the doorstep ritual stays on the 31st. A few municipalities set official trick-or-treat hours, and some : particularly in parts of the Midwest : occasionally shift "Beggars' Night" for weather or calendar reasons. But the holiday itself has proved immovable.

Halloween 2026: a Saturday with an extra hour

This year families get the calendar they've been asking for. Halloween 2026 falls on Saturday, October 31 : the first Saturday Halloween since 2020, and the last until 2037. Two other details make it unusual:

  • Daylight saving time ends at 2 a.m. on Sunday, November 1. Clocks fall back an hour, so Halloween night is effectively 25 hours long in most of the U.S. Since 2007, DST has ended on the first Sunday of November, which means the time change occasionally collides with Halloween weekend.
  • The moon will be waning. October's full Hunter's Moon falls on October 26, so by Halloween night the moon is a bright waning gibbous : plenty of moonlight for a spooky walk.

Expect bigger crowds at parades and haunted attractions, earlier trick-or-treating start times, and more neighborhood parties. Retailers expect a strong year as well: the National Retail Federation projected $13.5 billion in Halloween spending for 2026.

What Halloween means today

Ask ten people what Halloween means and you'll hear ten answers. That variety is part of its strength. A few meanings run through almost every version of the holiday:

Remembering the dead

The All Souls connection survives in the holiday's ghosts and graveyards, and in related traditions such as Mexico's Día de los Muertos on November 1–2. Halloween is one of the few times modern culture talks openly : if playfully : about death.

Playing with fear

Haunted houses, horror films and scary costumes let people experience fear in a controlled, shared way. Psychologists often describe it as a safe rehearsal for real anxiety, with friends close by.

Neighborliness

Trick-or-treating is one of the last rituals in which strangers knock on each other's doors and are welcomed. For many neighborhoods it is the single night of the year when everyone is outside at once : a theme explored in Halloween Block Parties.

Creativity

From carved pumpkins to homemade costumes, Halloween rewards making things. That impulse is behind the free pumpkin carving studio and other Festival Designers on this site.

The traditions tied to the date

Most Halloween traditions only make sense on one specific night:

Trick-or-treating Descends from souling and guising on All Hallows' Eve
Jack-o'-lanterns Lanterns to guide the living and ward off wandering spirits
Costumes Disguises to blend in with spirits abroad on the threshold night
Bonfires Samhain fires that marked the start of winter
Apple games Allhallowtide divination about love and the year ahead
Ghost stories The night when the dead were believed to be closest

The bottom line

Halloween is on October 31 because it is the eve of All Saints' Day, and because Samhain : counted from sunset : began on that same evening. Those two ancient calendars locked the date in place long before anyone thought to move it to a weekend. In 2026, at least, nobody needs to: the night falls on a Saturday, with an extra hour to enjoy it.

Questions & answers

Why isn't Halloween moved to a weekend?

Halloween's date is tied to All Saints' Day on November 1 and to centuries of tradition. Petitions to move it to the last Saturday of October have gathered signatures but no government or national body has adopted the change, so communities that want a weekend event simply hold parties or trunk-or-treats on the nearest Saturday.

What day of the week is Halloween 2026?

Halloween 2026 falls on Saturday, October 31. Daylight saving time ends at 2 a.m. on Sunday, November 1, so most Americans get an extra hour that night.

Is Halloween a federal holiday?

No. Halloween is not a federal or state holiday in the United States. Schools and businesses stay open, which is one reason weekday Halloweens tend to start later in the evening.

What is the difference between Halloween and Day of the Dead?

Halloween is on October 31 and grew from Celtic and British folk customs. Día de los Muertos is a Mexican tradition observed mainly on November 1 and 2, centered on welcoming and honoring deceased family members with ofrendas, marigolds and food.

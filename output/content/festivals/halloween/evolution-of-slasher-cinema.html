The slasher film genre has become one of the most iconic in horror, featuring a menacing killer, a group of victims, a series of increasing attacks, and a final showdown between the survivor and the threat. This formula has always evolved and changed over time.

Before the slasher formula hardened

Movies like Psycho, Peeping Tom, and Black Christmas laid the foundation for slasher films by introducing themes of voyeurism, vulnerable homes, faceless threats, and subjective camera angles. While not a perfect match to later slashers, they set crucial visual and storytelling standards.

The late-1970s and 1980s boom

Halloween showcased the power of atmosphere, perspective, and suburban mundanity in creating intense suspense. Following in its footsteps, Friday the 13th and numerous copycats transformed this formula into a successful franchise format, complete with iconic antagonists, established guidelines, and intricate scenarios.

Self-awareness changes the genre

In the 1990s, viewers were familiar with the traditional conventions of film genres. As movies started to acknowledge and play with these norms, genre literacy became a key element in creating suspense. Newer slasher films combined nostalgia with fresh insights on gender, identity, technology, and violence.

Why the genre keeps returning

Slashers serve as adaptable vessels for societal fears, with the murderer embodying threats to safety, skepticism towards authority, and apprehension towards the unknown. These narratives evolve with each generation, reflecting contemporary anxieties.

Frequently asked questions

What makes a movie a slasher?

Different interpretations exist, but generally, the term refers to a serial murderer who targets multiple victims in a suspenseful narrative.

Are slashers only about gore?

Some people depend on violence, but others are known for their use of suspense, sound design, perspective, and the concept of danger in everyday settings.

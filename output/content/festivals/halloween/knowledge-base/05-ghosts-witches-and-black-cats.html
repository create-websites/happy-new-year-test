Ghosts, Witches and Black Cats

Sheets with eyeholes, pointy hats, arched black cats. Every Halloween icon is a compressed piece of folklore : some ancient, some surprisingly modern.

At a glance

Halloween's main symbols each carry centuries of folklore. Ghosts reflect Samhain and All Souls beliefs that the dead return at the turning of the year, and the white-sheet costume echoes burial shrouds. The witch image combines European folk magic, the witch trials of the 1400s–1700s and 19th-century illustration, which popularized the pointed hat and broomstick. Black cats were linked to witches as familiars in Europe, although in Britain and Japan they are considered lucky. Bats, skeletons, spiders and owls added their own associations with night, death and omens.

Halloween has a visual vocabulary as recognizable as any language. Draw a crescent moon, a cat with an arched back and a figure on a broomstick, and nobody needs a caption. These images feel timeless, but each one has its own history : part ancient belief, part historical tragedy, part Victorian marketing.

Key takeaways

  • Ghosts reflect Samhain and All Souls beliefs; the white sheet echoes the burial shroud.
  • The witch image blends European folk magic, the witch trials and 19th-century illustration that fixed the pointed hat.
  • Black cats were seen as witches' familiars in parts of Europe : but are good luck in Britain and Japan.
  • Bats, skeletons, spiders and owls joined the cast through bonfires, memento mori art and old omens.

Ghosts: the returning dead

Ghosts are the oldest Halloween symbol and the most direct link to the holiday's roots. In the Gaelic world, Samhain marked the turn of the year when the otherworld was thought to lie close. In the Christian calendar, All Souls' Day (November 2) was devoted to the dead, and folk belief across Europe held that souls might return to visit their former homes on the night before.

Families sometimes left food or set an extra place at the table for departed relatives, a gesture of welcome echoed today in Mexico's Día de los Muertos.

Why a white sheet?

The classic Halloween ghost : a white sheet with two eyeholes : has a grim logic. For centuries the dead were buried in shrouds or winding sheets, so a returning spirit was naturally imagined wrapped in one. Stage productions of the 1800s used white drapery for ghosts, and the sheet's real secret weapon was cost: it was the costume anyone could make in five minutes. The NRF's 2026 survey still ranks "ghost" among the top five costumes for both children and adults.

Witches: from village healers to Halloween icons

The Halloween witch is a composite figure built over centuries.

Folk magic. Across Europe, village "cunning folk" offered herbal remedies, charms and fortune-telling. Most were tolerated : even valued.

The witch trials. Between roughly the 1400s and the 1700s, fear of diabolical witchcraft fueled waves of persecution. Tens of thousands of people, the majority of them women, were executed in Europe. In colonial America, the Salem witch trials of 1692 ended with 20 people executed : a tragedy explored in Halloween in Salem, Massachusetts.

The storybook witch. By the 19th century the witch had become a figure of fairy tales and cartoons. Illustrators settled on the now-standard look: an old woman with a pointed hat, a cloak, a broomstick and a cauldron. Halloween postcards of the early 1900s spread that image widely, and The Wizard of Oz (1939) gave it green skin.

The pointed hat

The origin of the tall conical hat is genuinely debated. Theories include fashionable tall hats of the 1600s, pointed medieval headwear, and simple artistic convention. What's certain is that by the late 1800s it was the universal shorthand for "witch."

The broomstick

The broom was a household object closely tied to women's domestic work. Early modern trial records and images also describe witches flying on staffs, brooms or animals to secret gatherings. The broomstick survived as the witch's vehicle of choice.

Today the witch is embraced rather than feared. "Witch" was the top adult costume in the NRF's 2026 survey, and many people now view the figure as a symbol of independence and nature-based spirituality.

Black cats: unlucky, or the luckiest of all?

Cats were long associated with the night and with mystery. In parts of medieval and early modern Europe they became tied to witchcraft : black cats in particular were said to be familiars, animal spirits serving a witch, or even witches in disguise. That association carried black cats straight into the Halloween iconography of the 1800s.

But superstition depends on where you live:

Place Black cat belief
United States and much of Europe Crossing your path is bad luck
Britain and Ireland A black cat arriving at your home brings good fortune
Scotland A strange black cat on your doorstep means prosperity
Japan Black cats are lucky, especially for single women
Sailors' lore A ship's black cat brought luck and safe return

Some animal shelters once paused black cat adoptions around Halloween out of concern for the animals' safety. Many have since dropped that policy, finding no evidence of harm and noting that black cats are often adopted less frequently than others.

Bats: creatures of the bonfire

Bats are active on autumn nights and feed on insects. One appealing theory holds that Samhain bonfires attracted swarms of insects, which in turn attracted bats swooping over the celebrations. Later, Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897) tied bats to vampires, and Hollywood's Dracula films made the connection unforgettable.

Skeletons and skulls

Skeletons belong to a long European tradition of memento mori : "remember that you will die." Medieval art depicted the danse macabre, in which skeletons lead people of every rank in a dance to the grave, a reminder that death comes to all. On Halloween the skeleton lost its sermon and gained a sense of humor; it is now as likely to be dancing on a lawn as looming in a graveyard. The 12-foot skeleton has become a modern icon of its own, as described in The Rise of Elaborate Halloween Houses.

Spiders, webs and owls

  • Spiders and cobwebs suggest abandoned, neglected places : the haunted house where no one has dusted for a century.
  • Owls have been seen as omens in many cultures. In parts of Europe an owl's call at night was thought to foretell death, while in ancient Greece the owl symbolized wisdom.
  • Crows and ravens, feeding on battlefields, became birds of ill omen in Celtic and Norse lore.

Colors: orange and black

Halloween's palette reflects its seasonal meaning. Orange stands for harvest, pumpkins and firelight; black for night, darkness and death. Purple and green, now common in décor, came later through costume and lighting design. See Elegant Halloween Decor for how to use the palette with style.

Folklore you can see on any porch

Next time you walk a Halloween street, look at the decorations as a kind of folklore museum. The sheet ghost speaks of shrouds and All Souls; the witch carries the memory of very real persecutions; the black cat argues about luck from one country to the next. Halloween keeps all of these stories alive : mostly by turning them into fun. For a porch that tells its own story, use the ideas in The Ultimate Halloween Home Decorating Guide.

Frequently asked questions

Why are black cats associated with Halloween?

In parts of medieval and early modern Europe, black cats were believed to be witches' familiars or witches in disguise. As witches became a Halloween symbol, the black cat came with them. In Britain, Ireland and Japan, however, black cats have long been considered lucky.

Why do witches wear pointed hats?

No one knows for certain. Historians point to tall hats worn in the 1600s, to pointed medieval headgear, and to 19th-century illustrators who drew witches in tall conical hats. The image was cemented by children's books and later by films such as The Wizard of Oz (1939).

Why are ghosts shown as white sheets?

The dead were traditionally buried wrapped in white shrouds or winding sheets, so a returning spirit was imagined in one. A sheet with eyeholes was also the cheapest possible costume, which helped it become a Halloween classic.

Why are bats a Halloween symbol?

Bats are active at night in autumn, and bonfires once attracted insects that drew bats close to celebrations. Their link to vampires, popularized by Bram Stoker's Dracula in 1897, reinforced the connection.

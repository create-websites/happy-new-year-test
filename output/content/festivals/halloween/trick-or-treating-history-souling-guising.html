Trick-or-Treating Through the Ages: Souling, Guising and Candy Runs

Modern trick-or-treating feels simple: wear a costume, knock on a door and collect candy. Its history is much more interesting, combining older door-to-door customs with community efforts to turn Halloween mischief into a structured family ritual.

Souling and seasonal visiting

In parts of medieval and early modern Europe, poorer people and children sometimes went door to door around All Souls’ observances asking for food in exchange for prayers or songs. These customs varied by region and should not be treated as a single direct ancestor, but they show that ritual visiting around the season has deep roots.

Guising brings performance to the doorstep

In Scotland and Ireland, “guisers” wore disguises and offered songs, jokes, recitations or tricks in return for food or coins. The emphasis on performance helps explain why costumes and doorstep exchange fit so naturally together in later Halloween practice.

The North American neighborhood ritual

As Halloween grew in the United States and Canada, communities experimented with parties, parades and organized children’s activities. During the twentieth century, especially after World War II, candy became inexpensive and widely marketed, suburban neighborhoods expanded and trick-or-treating settled into the familiar format used today.

Making the tradition more inclusive

Many communities now use allergy-aware treats, non-food options, sensory-friendly hours and “trunk-or-treat” events. The best modern version keeps the social ritual—neighbors opening doors to children—while making participation safer and easier for different families.

Frequently asked questions

When did “trick or treat” become a common phrase?

The phrase became increasingly visible in North American newspapers and popular culture during the early twentieth century, with the custom expanding strongly in the postwar era.

Is trick-or-treating the same everywhere?

No. Timing, age expectations, candy customs and community rules vary widely by country, city and neighborhood.

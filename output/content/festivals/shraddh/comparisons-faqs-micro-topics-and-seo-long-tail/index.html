Most questions about Pitru Paksha are not about the big picture but about specifics. What is the difference between tarpan and Shraddha? Is Mahalaya the same as Sarva Pitru Amavasya? We missed the tithi, what now? Can we hold an engagement this week? What should I wear to my friend's family Shraddha?

This guide collects those questions in one place. The first half compares commonly confused terms and handles special situations; the second half offers 100 quick answers and a 50-term glossary that you can use as a reference while reading any other guide on this site.

Answers here describe common practice. Your family's custom, your region and your priest's tradition come first where they differ.
Pitru Paksha 2026 — New Delhi reference: Purnima Shraddha falls on Saturday, 26 September 2026; the main Krishna Paksha sequence begins with Pratipada Shraddha on Sunday, 27 September; and Sarva Pitru Amavasya falls on Saturday, 10 October. Chaturthi and Panchami are both assigned to 30 September in this reference calendar. View the complete 2026 calendar and location notes. Outside India, use a Panchang set to the city where the observance will be performed because the civil date can shift.

Key takeaways

  • Shraddha is the whole rite; tarpan (water and sesame) and pind daan (rice balls) are parts of it or stand-alone offerings.
  • Mahalaya Amavasya and Sarva Pitru Amavasya are the same day: the new moon that closes Pitru Paksha (10 October 2026 in the New Delhi reference).
  • A missed or unknown tithi is traditionally covered on Sarva Pitru Amavasya.
  • Pitru Paksha is a time of restraint: weddings, housewarmings and major purchases are commonly postponed.
  • After a recent death, first-year rites follow their own schedule; ask your priest before adding a Mahalaya Shraddha.

Practice varies

This is a general cultural guide, not a substitute for instruction from your family elders, priest or sampradaya. Ritual sequences, dates and customs differ across regions and households; where they do, the family’s own tradition is the one to follow.

Shraddha vs Tarpan: What Is the Difference?

The two words are often used interchangeably, but they are not the same thing.

MeaningA rite performed with shraddha (faith) for ancestors"Satisfying" or "gratifying"; offering water
ScopeA complete ceremony: sankalpa, invocation, offerings, pinda, food, feeding a Brahmin or guests, charityOne act: offering water mixed with sesame, usually with kusha
DurationAn hour or more with a priest10 to 30 minutes

Tarpan is usually part of Shraddha, but it can also be done on its own, which is why many families who cannot perform a full Shraddha still offer tarpan every day of Pitru Paksha.

Shraddha vs Pind Daan: What Is the Difference?

Pind daan is the offering of pindas, balls usually made of cooked rice or barley flour mixed with sesame, ghee, honey or milk, to ancestors. Shraddha is the full rite in which pind daan is commonly one component.

  • In a home Shraddha, pinda offering is part of the standard sequence in many traditions, though some simplified home rites omit it.
  • At pilgrimage sites, especially Gaya, pind daan is the central act, and pilgrims often speak of "doing pind daan" rather than "doing Shraddha".
  • Symbolism: the pinda is widely understood as nourishing the ancestor and as a sign of the continuing bond of lineage (the word sapinda, sharing the pinda, describes close kin).

Pitru Paksha vs Mahalaya: What Is the Difference?

Pitru Paksha is the fortnight; Mahalaya can mean either the whole fortnight or its final day, depending on region.

  • In much of North India, "Pitru Paksha" or "Shraadh" names the fortnight, and the last day is Sarva Pitru Amavasya.
  • In South India and Maharashtra, the fortnight is often called Mahalaya Paksha, and the last day Mahalaya Amavasya.
  • In Bengal, "Mahalaya" usually means the final day itself, marked by pre-dawn tarpan and the start of Devi Paksha leading to Durga Puja.

So all three regions are talking about the same period; the vocabulary differs. See Mahalaya in Bengal vs Sarva Pitru Amavasya elsewhere.

Mahalaya Amavasya vs Sarva Pitru Amavasya: Are They the Same?

Yes. Both names refer to the new moon (Amavasya) at the end of Pitru Paksha. In the New Delhi 2026 reference it falls on Saturday, 10 October. Outside India, check a Panchang set to the city where the observance is performed.

  • Sarva Pitru means "all ancestors": on this day Shraddha can be performed for every ancestor, including those whose tithi is unknown or was missed.
  • Mahalaya is the name used in Bengal, the South and Maharashtra.
  • Other regional names include Peddala Amavasya in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana.

See Sarva Pitru Amavasya 2026 and Mahalaya Amavasya 2026.

Pitru Paksha vs Amavasya Shraddha: How Are They Related?

Amavasya, the new moon, is associated with ancestors throughout the year, not only in Pitru Paksha.

  • Monthly Amavasya tarpan (sometimes called darsha shraddha) is observed by many families, especially in South India, on every new moon.
  • Pitru Paksha is the most important annual period for ancestors, ending with the most important Amavasya of the year for ancestral rites.
  • Other Amavasyas carry special ancestral weight in particular regions, such as Aadi Amavasai in Tamil Nadu and Karkidaka Vavu in Kerala (both in July to August), and Thai Amavasai in Tamil Nadu (January to February).
  • Traditional lists of the year's Shraddha occasions (sometimes counted as 96) include every Amavasya.

Tarpan at Home vs Riverbank: Traditional Context and Practical Differences

Riverbanks and tirthas have long been favoured for tarpan, because water is the medium of the offering and sacred rivers are thought to carry it. But home tarpan is equally part of tradition.

Can Shraddha Be Performed on a Different Day If the Tithi Is Missed?

Tradition provides for this. The most common options:

  1. Sarva Pitru Amavasya. The main provision: Shraddha can be performed on the final day for any ancestor whose tithi was missed.
  2. The corresponding tithi in the next month or another Amavasya, according to some priests.
  3. The annual death-anniversary Shraddha (on the tithi of death in its lunar month), which is separate from Pitru Paksha and remains the other key annual occasion.

A tithi can also be "lost" in the calendar itself when two tithis fall on the same sunrise, as with Chaturthi and Panchami on 30 September 2026 in the New Delhi reference. In such cases Panchangs specify which Shraddha is observed on which day. See how the correct tithi is chosen.

What If You Miss Pitru Paksha Shraddha?

Missing a Shraddha is common, through illness, travel, work or simple forgetting, and tradition treats it calmly.

  • Missed the tithi: perform Shraddha or tarpan on Sarva Pitru Amavasya.
  • Missed the whole fortnight: many priests suggest a Shraddha or tarpan on a later Amavasya, feeding the needy, charity in the ancestors' names, or observing the annual death-anniversary Shraddha with care.
  • Could not do the full rite: a simple tarpan, a meal given to someone in need, and a moment of remembrance are meaningful.
Be cautious of anyone who presents a missed Shraddha as a cause of misfortune that requires expensive remedies. Classical and popular teaching frames Shraddha as an act of gratitude and duty.

What If You Do Not Know the Ancestor’s Tithi or Gotra?

Unknown tithi

  • If you know the date of death (Gregorian), a priest or Panchang can usually work out the tithi.
  • If not, perform the Shraddha on Sarva Pitru Amavasya, which is for all ancestors.
  • Some families use a tithi by special category, such as Navami for mothers and married women who died before their husbands (Matru Navami), where that custom applies.

Unknown gotra

  • Many priests use Kashyapa gotra when the family gotra is unknown.
  • Others use only names and relationships.

See Shraddha when the death date is unknown and unknown gotra.

Can You Perform Shraddha for More Than One Ancestor on the Same Day?

Yes. It happens constantly, for three reasons:

  • Standard structure: a parvana Shraddha honours three generations on the paternal side (father, grandfather, great-grandfather) and, in many traditions, three on the maternal side, together.
  • Shared tithis: two ancestors who died on the same lunar tithi are honoured on the same day.
  • Sarva Pitru Amavasya: all ancestors can be honoured together.

In practice, each ancestor is named in the sankalpa, and separate water offerings, and sometimes separate pindas, are made for each. The meal is usually prepared once. See Shraddha for multiple ancestors.

12 of 20

Can Shraddha Be Performed for Maternal Ancestors?

Yes. Maternal ancestors are included in several ways.

  • Parvana Shraddha in many traditions includes the maternal grandfather, great-grandfather and great-great-grandfather (with their wives) alongside the paternal line.
  • Mahalaya Shraddha is often described as including a wide circle of relatives: parents, grandparents on both sides, uncles, aunts, siblings, teachers and friends.
  • Matamaha Shraddha, also called Dauhitra Pratipada, is observed in many calendars on Ashwin Shukla Pratipada, the day after Pitru Paksha ends, when a daughter's son honours his maternal grandfather.
  • Matru Navami honours mothers and married women of the family.

See Shraddha for mother, father and other ancestors.

13 of 20

Can Shraddha Be Performed for Someone Who Died Recently?

This depends heavily on tradition, and it is one question where a priest's guidance really matters.

  • First-year rites come first. After a death, families follow a sequence of rites: the rites of the first days, monthly observances in many traditions, and sapindikarana, the rite that joins the deceased with the ancestors. Some families perform sapindikarana around the 12th or 13th day, others at one year.
  • Before sapindikarana, the deceased is often honoured through ekoddishta rites (for one person) rather than being included in the collective parvana Shraddha of Pitru Paksha.
  • The first Pitru Paksha after a death is treated differently across communities: some perform a special Shraddha, some wait until after the first anniversary, and some include the person on Sarva Pitru Amavasya.
  • The first annual Shraddha (barsi or varshika) falls on the tithi of death one lunar year later.

If you are grieving a recent loss, the fortnight can be tender. Simple remembrance, prayer and charity are always appropriate while you seek guidance. See remembering ancestors beyond ritual.

What Should You Wear During Shraddha?

Simplicity is the common thread.

  • Performer (karta): in many traditions a clean, unstitched dhoti and upper cloth for men; plain, clean clothes if that is not possible. Women performers commonly wear a simple saree or salwar suit.
  • Colours: white, off-white, cream or muted colours are common. Bright red, black and flashy prints are often avoided, though customs differ.
  • New clothes: traditionally not worn during Pitru Paksha; wear clean, older garments.
  • Sacred thread: men who wear the yajnopavita shift it to the right shoulder (apasavya or prachinaviti) for ancestral offerings, as the priest instructs.
  • Leather and accessories: many remove leather belts and shoes and keep jewellery minimal.
  • Guests: modest, clean, subdued clothing; no need for anything special. See guest etiquette.

What Should You Avoid During Pitru Paksha?

Restrictions vary a great deal, and many urban families observe only some. Commonly mentioned:

  • Celebrations: weddings, engagements, housewarmings, naming and first-haircut ceremonies. See weddings during Pitru Paksha.
  • New purchases: new clothes, jewellery, vehicles, property; some families extend this to any major purchase.
  • Grooming: some men do not cut hair, shave or trim nails, especially the performer.
  • Food: onion, garlic, meat, fish, eggs and alcohol are avoided by many; some also avoid specific foods such as certain lentils, brinjal or bottle gourd depending on region. See foods commonly avoided.
  • Conduct: quarrels, harsh words and turning away anyone who comes for food are especially discouraged.
Many families, particularly abroad, keep the dietary and conduct customs and relax the rest. That is a family decision, not a failure of observance.

Can Weddings or Auspicious Ceremonies Be Planned During Pitru Paksha?

Traditionally, no: Pitru Paksha is considered inauspicious for new beginnings, and wedding muhurtas are not normally set in it.

  • What is usually postponed: weddings, engagements, griha pravesh (housewarming), mundan, upanayana, business openings and major purchases.
  • What usually continues: daily worship, work, study, travel, medical care and ordinary life.
  • Why: the fortnight belongs to the ancestors; attention is meant to turn towards remembrance rather than celebration.
  • After it ends: the next day begins Navratri (Ghatasthapana on the local 2026 Navratri date in India), a festive and auspicious period.

Couples abroad who must schedule a civil ceremony during the fortnight sometimes do the legal registration then and hold religious ceremonies later. Ask your family priest about muhurtas.

Why Is Pitru Paksha Considered a Period for Remembrance Rather Than Celebration?

Several threads of tradition come together here.

  • Time given to ancestors: in traditional understanding, this fortnight is when ancestors are especially close to the living world and receive offerings; attention belongs to them.
  • Rhythm of the year: the fortnight of the ancestors is followed immediately by the fortnight of the goddess (Devi Paksha and Navratri), so restraint gives way to festivity.
  • Gratitude and debt: the idea of pitru rina, a debt to ancestors, frames the period as one of duty and thanks. See Pitru Rina.
  • Humility: simple food, simple dress and postponed pleasures are ways of setting aside the self.

It is sombre, but not only sad. Many families remember it as a warm time of stories, favourite dishes of grandparents and food shared with others.

How Should a Guest Behave at a Shraddha Meal or Ritual?

Being invited to a Shraddha, as a relative, friend or the honoured guest who represents the ancestors, is a mark of trust.

  • Arrive on time and quietly. Rituals often begin at a set time in the late morning.
  • Dress simply in clean, subdued clothes. See what to wear.
  • Do not bring flowers, sweets or gifts meant for celebrations unless the host asks. A donation in the ancestors' name is a thoughtful alternative.
  • Follow the host's lead on seating, removing shoes and where to stand.
  • Photographs: ask before taking any.
  • Eating: if you are served as a guest who represents the ancestors, eat calmly and without criticism; it is customary to accept the food offered. Mention dietary needs discreetly in advance.
  • Conversation: gentle, respectful and about the person remembered. Avoid jokes and arguments.
  • Leaving: thank the host; there is no need for festive farewells.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between Shraddha and tarpan?

Shraddha is the complete ancestral rite; tarpan is the offering of water with sesame, usually part of Shraddha but also performed on its own.

Are Mahalaya Amavasya and Sarva Pitru Amavasya the same?

Yes. Both names refer to the new moon that ends Pitru Paksha, which falls on 10 October 2026 in the New Delhi reference.

What should I do if I missed my ancestor's Shraddha tithi?

Perform Shraddha or tarpan on Sarva Pitru Amavasya, which is traditionally meant for all ancestors, including those whose tithi was missed or is unknown.

Can weddings be held during Pitru Paksha?

Traditionally no. Weddings, engagements, housewarmings and other auspicious ceremonies are commonly postponed until after Pitru Paksha.

Can Shraddha be performed for someone who died recently?

First-year rites such as sapindikarana follow their own schedule, and communities differ on whether a separate Pitru Paksha Shraddha is performed in the first year. Ask your family priest.

What should a guest wear to a Shraddha?

Clean, simple clothes in white or muted colours, without festive jewellery or bright prints.

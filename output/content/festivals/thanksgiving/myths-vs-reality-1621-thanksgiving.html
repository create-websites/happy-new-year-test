Thanksgiving 1621: Myths, Reality and Wampanoag Perspectives

Separate the familiar Thanksgiving story from the 1621 harvest gathering, Wampanoag diplomacy and Indigenous perspectives that complicate the myth.

Read the guideAll 20 articles
🍁 ThanksgivingHistory, Heritage & Perspectives

What the surviving record actually describes

The event commonly called the “first Thanksgiving” was a three-day harvest gathering at Plymouth in 1621 involving English colonists and Ousamequin (Massasoit) with about ninety Wampanoag men. The surviving English account is brief. It describes fowling, recreation and the Wampanoag bringing five deer. It does not describe the tidy school-pageant scene that later popular culture created.

Calling the gathering the first Thanksgiving can also obscure older traditions. Wampanoag communities had long-standing ceremonies of gratitude connected to harvests and the natural world, while English communities observed days of thanksgiving and prayer before 1621. The modern U.S. holiday developed much later.

Alliance, survival and political context

The meal happened within a political relationship, not in a vacuum. Ousamequin had entered a mutual-protection agreement with the Plymouth colonists. Wampanoag assistance, including agricultural knowledge and diplomacy, mattered to the colony’s survival, while the alliance also served Wampanoag strategic interests in a changing regional landscape.

That temporary cooperation should not be used to erase what followed: expanding English settlement, land loss, warfare and profound disruption for Native communities. A responsible Thanksgiving history can acknowledge both the 1621 encounter and the longer consequences of colonization.

How the national story was built

The 1621 gathering was not immediately repeated as a national holiday. Nineteenth-century writers and editors helped elevate it into an origin story for the United States, while Thanksgiving proclamations and family customs gradually created the holiday’s modern shape.

For families and classrooms today, the most useful approach is not to replace one oversimplified story with another. It is to distinguish evidence, later mythology and living Indigenous perspectives—and to recognize that Native nations remain present, diverse and contemporary.

A better way to talk about Thanksgiving

Use specific names such as Wampanoag rather than generic labels. Avoid costumes and classroom activities that reduce living cultures to stereotypes. When discussing 1621, explain who was there, what sources actually say, and why the event has different meanings for different communities.

Frequently asked questions

Was the 1621 gathering really the first Thanksgiving?

It is better described as a 1621 harvest gathering. Both Indigenous peoples and Europeans had older traditions of giving thanks, and the modern U.S. Thanksgiving holiday developed much later.

Were the Wampanoag simply invited dinner guests?

The historical relationship was political and strategic as well as social. Ousamequin and the Plymouth colonists had formed an alliance, and Wampanoag participants brought deer to the gathering.

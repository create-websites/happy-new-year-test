National Day of Mourning: Indigenous Perspectives on Thanksgiving

Learn about the history and significance of the National Day of Mourning in Plymouth, which began in 1970 and why Thanksgiving serves as a day of remembrance and activism for many Indigenous people.

🍁 ThanksgivingHistory, Heritage & Perspectives

A different meaning on the same day

For over 50 years, the United American Indians of New England (UAINE) have hosted a National Day of Mourning in Plymouth, Massachusetts, on U.S. Thanksgiving. UAINE views this day as a time to pay tribute to Indigenous ancestors and their strength, reflect on the impacts of colonization, and speak out against persistent racism and oppression.

This observation coexists with, rather than simply mirrors, the family-focused Thanksgiving that many Americans celebrate. It demonstrates how a single national holiday can hold drastically varying historical connotations.

How the observance began

UAINE links the inaugural National Day of Mourning to Wamsutta Frank James, an Aquinnah Wampanoag leader who was asked to speak at a 1970 event marking the Pilgrims' landing. However, event planners disapproved of his planned speech, which criticized traditional narratives of colonial history.

The protest evolved into a yearly Indigenous-led assembly at Cole's Hill in Plymouth.

Why language and attribution matter

When discussing the National Day of Mourning, it is crucial to credit the perspectives of those individuals and groups sharing them. UAINE defines Thanksgiving as a solemn reminder of genocide, land theft, and cultural erasure for numerous Native individuals. This is their stated viewpoint and the reason for their commemoration.

Do not generalize all Native nations as having a singular viewpoint. Indigenous communities exhibit diversity, with individuals holding varying perspectives on Thanksgiving.

What respectful learning can look like

Families and schools can integrate gratitude exercises with learning about local history: discover the original inhabitants of your area, explore Native-authored works, back modern Native groups, and steer clear of stereotypical "Pilgrim and Indian" outfits. The aim is to promote historical understanding, not guilt-tripping or idealization.

Frequently asked questions

When did the National Day of Mourning begin?

UAINE has organized the observance in Plymouth since 1970.

Do all Native Americans view Thanksgiving in the same way?

Indigenous nations and individuals are not homogenous; the National Day of Mourning reflects a unique Indigenous tradition and varied perspectives, rather than a singular Native viewpoint.
